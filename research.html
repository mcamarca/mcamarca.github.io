Thermal Properties of Callisto with ALMA

The material properties of a surface can be inferred by observing the thermal emission from that surface. Using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), we observed the thermal emission of Jupiter's moon Callisto to constrain its thermal properties. This project produced the first global map of Callisto's thermal properties (think thermal inertia and emissivity) at millimeter wavelengths. (Camarca et al. 2025 ↗, Camarca et al. 2023 ↗)

The data at left shows that some parts of Callisto's surface are colder than thermal models predict, and other regions are warmer. By linking these warm and cold spots with local geology, we can infer what causes the thermal anomaly. In this case, Callisto's central cold spot is co-located with a large, icy impact basin called Valhalla. The warm region around it is likely due to micrometeorites texturizing the surface, allowing it to warm up more quickly. These kinds of observations are possible because ALMA has incredible spatial resolution and sensitivity at millimeter wavelengths.

Surface ice chemistry of Callisto with JWST

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) is equipped with instruments that allow us to probe the spatial distribution and quantities of ices across a planetary surface. Understanding what ices are on Callisto and where they are most abundant can help us understand how Callisto's ancient surface has evolved. For our work on mapping Callisto's surface volatiles, please see (Cartwright et al. 2024) ↗

At the right is a map of the amount of CO2 ice on Callisto's trailing hemisphere. There is far more CO2 on Callisto's trailing hemisphere because energetic particles in Jupiter's magnetosphere preferentially bombard this hemisphere. Check out the paper linked above for the band depth maps I produced.

The Callisto JWST NIRSpec dataset is quite rich, and there is more science to come! Stay tuned for my upcoming paper with more spatial coverage and new maps of the crystalline ice feature.

Twilight Program

There are many kinds of planetary phenomena that can change on just a few weeks or days notice. These include events like volcanic eruptions on Jupiter's moon Io or clouds on Saturn's moon Titan. There is a long-running program at the Keck observatory that allows observers the chance to use some of their time for monitoring bright solar system objects. I currently am part of the Twilight Zone team and am currently responsible for the image reduction. Most recent data was taken using the NIRC2 infrared imager at Keck. See the Twilight Zone website ↗ for images of Io, Titan, Uranus, and Neptune.

Image: A NIRC2 infrared image of Io taken in M band (~4.5-4.7 µm). The bright points on its surface are hotspots associated with volcanic activity. Data taken on July 18th, 2024.

Galilean satellite aurora

The satellites of Jupiter have aurora just like the Earth does! Jupiter's very strong magnetosphere is capable of exciting oxygen in the (thin) atmospheres of the icy satellites, producing the familiar red and green auroras we see on Earth. Understanding the aurora on these satellites can help us diagnose their atmospheric constituents. The paper from our group: (de Kleer et al. 2023) ↗

This is a project I've contributed to by helping out with several nights of Keck/HIRES observations. The observing technique is quite cool! Because the auroral lines are very weak, we must look for them when the moons are in Jupiter's shadow. However, once the object goes into Jupiter's shadow, it's difficult to track, so we constantly nod back and forth between a satellite in eclipse and a non-eclipsing satellite to check the tracking. We most recently conducted observations of Europa in eclipse one night during the October 2024 DPS meeting in Boise, ID.

The New York Times ↗ published a nice write-up of this science.

Cometary atmospheres

Comets are like postcards from the young solar system. Most of them spend their lives in cold storage far from the Sun, but when they embark on a journey through the inner solar system, their delicate ices begin to sublimate and fluoresce, putting on a dazzling, and scientifically useful, spectacle. Using the NIRSPEC instrument at the Keck telescope, we conducted a molecular inventory of comet 252P/LINEAR (Paganini et al. 2019) ↗

This project was my first project in planetary science. I started this research as a 2017 summer intern at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center in Greenbelt, MD, and it continued until I left to start my Ph.D. at Caltech. For additional samples of my contributions to cometary science, see Faggi et al. 2021 ↗ and Lippi et al. 2020 ↗

A Game for Organic Chemistry Education

Organic chemistry is a notoriously difficult undergraduate course with high attrition rates. As a biology major at Marymount University in Arlington, VA, I tutored the organic chemistry sequence in our department. In collaboration with my undergraduate thesis advisor, Dr. Deana Jaber, we developed a card game to help students learn the substitution and elimination reactions of alcohols, an important topic usually covered in second-year organic chemistry. We tested the game with active organic chemistry students in the department, and this research formed the basis of my undergraduate honors thesis, which we published in the Journal of Chemical Education (Camarca et al. 2019) ↗

As a follow-up project, we published a literature review of all published games/case studies for organic chemistry educators. (Camarca et al. 2022) ↗

3D Printed Prosthetics

As an undergraduate, I did a small research project on testing 3D printed prosthetics designed for children. This was in the earlier days of 3D printing technology, and the field has come a long way since. My task for this project was to test the usability of designs available from the ENABLE community. I found that some hand designs were quite difficult to manufacture because the built-in scaffolding on some of the designs was not optimized for easy removal. We provided this feedback to the prosthetics designers so that better prints could be designed. As an additional project, I updated a hand design in the lab to incorporate touch-screen functionality. Although my current research direction into planetary astronomy is quite different than 3D printed prosthetics, I'm thankful for those summer days spent in the Marymount physics lab as those were my first days of research. :)
